The 183 day rule and how tax residence is decided

More than half a year in one country usually makes you resident there. The counting rules, the tax year used and the tie breaker tests all differ, and that is where people get caught.

Check which tax year applies before counting. The UK, Australia and India all start theirs on different dates.

On this page

    Spend 183 days in a country and you are likely tax resident there. That is the rule most people have heard, and the part they usually get wrong is that it is one test among several, not the whole thing.

    What the 183 day test says

    Most countries treat physical presence of 183 days or more in a tax year as evidence of residence. 183 is simply more than half of 365, so it identifies the country where you spent the majority of the year.

    The complications start immediately after that sentence.

    • Which year. Some countries use the calendar year. The UK tax year runs 6 April to 5 April. Australia uses 1 July to 30 June. India uses 1 April to 31 March.
    • Which days count. Many systems count any day where you were present at midnight. Some count arrival and departure days, some exclude days in transit, some count part days.
    • Rolling versus fixed. The US substantial presence test looks at a weighted three year total, and Schengen visitors are measured on a rolling 180 day window rather than a fixed year.

    The US substantial presence formula

    The US counts all days in the current year, one third of the days in the prior year, and one sixth of the days in the year before that. If the total reaches 183 and you were present at least 31 days in the current year, you are a resident for tax purposes.

    Someone spending 120 days a year in the US every year gets 120 + 40 + 20, which is 180. Just under. Spend 125 days a year and the total is 187.5, which is over. The margin between visiting and being taxed on worldwide income is about five days a year, and plenty of people cross it without noticing.

    Keep a day log. Boarding passes, passport stamps and card transactions. Tax authorities in several countries now cross reference airline manifests, and the burden of proving where you were sits with you, not with them.

    Why counting days is not enough

    Leaving before day 183 does not automatically make you non resident. Most systems also look at where your permanent home is, where your family lives, where your economic interests sit, and where you habitually live. The UK's Statutory Residence Test can make you resident on as few as 16 days if you have enough connecting ties.

    The other half of the problem is that two countries can both claim you. That is what double tax treaties are for. Treaty tie breaker rules run in order: permanent home, then centre of vital interests, then habitual abode, then nationality, then agreement between the two authorities. Day counting only decides the entry to that process.

    The "nowhere resident" plan that circulates online, where someone spends under 183 days everywhere and claims to owe tax nowhere, fails on exactly these rules. Most countries keep taxing you until you establish residence somewhere else.

    The Schengen 90 in 180 rule

    Different system, same number, and it is the one most travellers get wrong. Visa free visitors can spend 90 days in any rolling 180 day period across the whole Schengen area. On any given day, look back 180 days and count the days you were inside the area. If the count exceeds 90, you are overstaying.

    It is not 90 days per country and it is not a fresh 90 days after leaving for a week. Days drop out of the window one at a time as they age past 180, so the allowance refills gradually rather than resetting. The same structure appears in the 90 day visitor allowance that most people think of as the limit.

    Other 180 day deadlines

    A 1031 property exchange in the US gives 45 days to identify replacement property and 180 days from the sale to complete, and both run at the same time rather than in sequence, so the 45 day identification window eats into the 180. Many passports must be valid for 6 months beyond the intended stay. Employment tribunals, warranty extensions and some insurance claim windows use 180 days. Corporate half year reporting lands here too, which is why it pairs with the seasonal planning cycle in most businesses.

    If you are near the line

    1. Count on the correct tax year for the specific country, not the calendar year by default.
    2. Check whether arrival and departure days count. On frequent short trips this alone can move the total by weeks.
    3. Keep evidence for at least six years, because that is how far back several authorities can look.
    4. Check the treaty before assuming you are safe, since day counts are only the first test.
    5. Get advice from a tax adviser in both countries if you are within about 20 days of a threshold. This is not the area to rely on a forum answer.

    Common questions

    Does spending 183 days in a country make you tax resident?

    In most systems it is strong evidence of residence, but it is one test among several. Home, family, economic ties and habitual residence also count, and some countries can treat you as resident on far fewer days.

    Do arrival and departure days count towards 183 days?

    It depends on the country. Many count any day where you were present at midnight, some count both travel days in full, and a few exclude days spent purely in transit.

    How does the Schengen 90 in 180 rule work?

    On any given day you look back over the previous 180 days and count the days spent inside the Schengen area. The total must not exceed 90. Days age out of the window gradually rather than resetting after a trip.

    Can you avoid tax residence by staying under 183 days everywhere?

    Generally no. Most countries continue to treat you as resident until you establish residence elsewhere, and treaty tie breaker rules use factors beyond day counts.
